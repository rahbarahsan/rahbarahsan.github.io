About

I grew up in Chittagong, moved to Dhaka for computer science at North South University, and came to Carleton in Ottawa for a research master's on intrusion detection. The question that started there still runs through my work. When one class dominates the data, how do you get a model to see the rest?

Since then I've worked on cloud data validation in banking, and built the assessment that decides which AI models a federal department can build on. Outside work I build things to learn new tools, from a meal planner to a squash game you can play in the browser, and I run small experiments on how to teach small models.

Deciding which AI models a public institution can build on

Before a team builds on a model, it now goes through one repeatable assessment. Provenance and licence checks, automated security scans, and red teaming that includes risks no public benchmark covers. What clears it lands in a catalogue teams check first.

The hardest risks to test were the ones nobody had written a benchmark for, so we wrote our own.

Homeroom, teaching small models on a fixed budget

A teacher model builds an exam, diagnoses where a small student model goes wrong, and writes targeted examples for it, with every request passing a budget check, a cache and a cost ledger. The question is whether a small model can learn reusable rules from a few dozen examples.

So far the adaptive teacher has not beaten plain bulk generation, though it used about 57% fewer training examples. The negative results are published with the code.

Research · Carleton University · 2021 · First author

Finding rare attacks in network traffic

In the standard intrusion dataset the rarest attack class has 52 training records out of almost 126,000. I compared classical models and neural networks across resampling methods, and judged every version by weighted F1 instead of accuracy. A follow-up paper generated new rare samples with a conditional GAN.

A high accuracy score said almost nothing about the attacks we cared about.

At work · Federal public service · Pilot

Live subtitles in six languages

Live events needed subtitles in several languages, full of in-house acronyms that public models have never seen. Speech goes to text, through a terminology layer, then to translation, fast enough to read along.

The models translated the words fine and got the acronyms wrong. A small terminology layer fixed what a bigger model couldn't.
